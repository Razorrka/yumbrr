# How to write the words

A working analysis of the four reference cards signed **(J.V.)**, and how to
build your own without it reading as an imitation. Short passages are quoted
for study; the poems belong to their author.

The app has this same guide in the **Craft** tab, plus a spark generator and
live notes on whatever you're drafting.

---

## 1. The form

> A photograph that is too dark and too grainy. Four to eight lines under it in
> plain type, wide leading, no title. Two or three words in link blue. Initials
> in the bottom corner. Nothing else.

That's it. It looks like a screenshot of a notes app, and the disguise is doing
real work: a screenshot is something you *find*, not something you were shown.
It arrives without a frame around it saying THIS IS A POEM, so nobody braces.

Everything below is a form the way a sonnet is a form. Learn it, then it's yours.

---

## 2. The engine

Every one of these poems runs on the same two strokes: **admit a deficiency,
then ask to be taught.**

> I was never good
> at being **human**.
>
> teach me how to be **seventeen**
> on the floor of someone else's bedroom.

The confession is what earns the imperative. Without it, *teach me how to be
seventeen* is a demand. With it, it's a plea, and you'll follow a plea anywhere.

The speaker is a **student of being alive**. That's the whole posture, and it's
why these land harder than ordinary sad poems. "I am sad" is a mood, and the
reader already has their own. "I was never issued the instructions" is a
*situation*, and a reader can stand inside a situation.

**Do this:** say the thing you cannot do. Then ask a person, out loud, to show
you how.

## 3. The twelve moves

### 01 · Never name the metaphor

All four cards are about not being human. Not one says *robot*, *android*, or
*machine*. The conceit is carried entirely by **materials**:

> but I will create
> the wet **architecture** of myself.
>
> solder every **wire**
> into the warm red animal
> **of a heart**.

*Solder. Wire. Architecture.* Three nouns, and the reader assembles the whole
idea themselves — which is exactly why they believe it. Write "like a robot"
and you've done the work for them, and it dies on the page.

### 02 · Land the abstraction on an object

Not *teach me to be young*. **Teach me how to be seventeen on the floor of
someone else's bedroom.** The abstraction opens the line; something physical,
specific and faintly embarrassing closes it.

> **Never end a line on a concept if you can end it on furniture.**

### 03 · The odd measurement

Feelings get handled as quantities with dials on them. It's how an outsider
would actually approach them, and it's devastating:

> The exact volume I have to **laugh**
> so that I can **stay** until morning.
>
> how much of myself
> to leave behind each **year**.

Reach for the calibration words — *the exact, how much, the correct amount, the
right number of, the precise* — and apply them to something with no units.

### 04 · Three, then break the three

Two items set a pattern. The third snaps it, and the snap is the meaning.

> What do you know about
> the softest part behind the **knee**?
> the **hinge** where the jaw
> **meets** the skull?
> The space between two **hands**?

Knee, jaw — both on one body. Then *the space between two hands*, which belongs
to no single body and isn't a body part at all. The entire poem is hiding in a
list, and it detonates on the last item.

### 05 · Collide a cold noun with a warm one

*Wet architecture.* *The warm red animal of a heart.* One word from the
workshop, one word that's alive and slightly gross. The friction is the voice.

Build a two-column list — machinery left, wet biology right — and pair across it
until something makes you flinch. (The app's **Sparks** button does exactly
this.)

### 06 · Break the line where the fragment lies

Read the first line on its own:

> I was never good

Full stop. Total self-indictment. Then the next line arrives and narrows it to
*at being human* — smaller, and somehow worse. Break where the fragment can
stand up alone and mean something crueller or wider than the finished sentence.
That half-second of wrong meaning is free, and almost nothing else is.

### 07 · Ordinary nouns as sacred objects

> Show me **sleepovers** and
> birthday cake.

Sleepovers. Birthday cake. The floor of a bedroom. Morning. The longing is never
named — it's loaded into objects a nine-year-old would recognise.

Don't write *belonging*. Write the cake.

### 08 · End on the morning after

These poems never close on the event. They close on surviving it: *so that I can
stay until morning*; *How to wake up the morning after*. The party isn't the
subject. The aftermath is.

### 09 · Ask what you can't answer

*Tell me what lives beneath skin.* The question keeps the speaker below the
reader — still learning, never lecturing. A poem that asks cannot be smug.

### 10 · Type like it's 2am

Look closely at the capitals: `I was never good`, then lowercase `teach me`
after a full stop, then `The exact volume` capitalised again. It's inconsistent,
and the inconsistency **is** the intimacy — it reads as something typed in the
dark, not something submitted.

Do not tidy this. Clean capitalisation makes it a poem. Broken capitalisation
makes it a message.

### 11 · The blue thread

The real trick, and easy to miss. Read only the blue words of each card:

```
human · seventeen · laugh · stay
sleepovers · year · morning
lives · knee · hinge · meets · hands
human · architecture · wire · of a heart
```

Every one is a second, compressed poem.

The highlighting isn't decoration and it isn't emphasis — it's a **skeleton**.
Highlight the words the poem would collapse without, then read them alone. If
they don't hold, you've highlighted the wrong ones. Two to four per card.

The app shows your blue thread live under the editor for exactly this reason.

### 12 · Stop early

Four to eight lines, two or three stanzas. A screenshot has an edge. Most drafts
get better when you delete the last line, because the last line is usually where
you explained it.

## 4. The photograph

The image is not an illustration. It's **evidence**, and it's always of an
absence.

- A birthday table, candles lit, nobody sitting at it.
- A cake nobody is cutting.
- A road at dusk, out of focus, shot from a moving car.
- Streamers and fairy lights with no one underneath them.

**Never photograph the subject of the poem. Photograph the room they left.**

Underexposed, out of focus, flash-blown, taken at the wrong moment. A
technically bad photo reads as a true one, and a true one is what the poem needs
standing behind it.

Source them from your own camera roll six years back — not a stock site. The
graininess is doing memory's job, so start from the flattest, ugliest photo you
have and let the **Flash** preset do the rest.

---

## 5. What kills it

- **Explaining.** One line of interpretation and the reader stops working.
- **Naming the feeling.** *Lonely, broken, empty, numb.* Give the volume of the laugh instead.
- **Rhyme.** It makes the voice performed. This voice is overheard.
- **Adjective stacks.** *Cold empty aching silence.* One good noun beats four adjectives.
- **A title.** These cards have none. A title tells the reader what to think first.
- **Neatness.** Perfect grammar, tidy ending. It stops sounding like a person at 2am.

---

## 6. A twenty-minute routine

1. Open your camera roll. Take the first photo with no people in it.
2. Write the deficiency: *I was never good at ___.* Don't think.
3. Ask to be taught it — and land the ask on a specific room at a specific hour.
4. Add one odd measurement.
5. Cut every adjective. Cut the last line.
6. Highlight two or three words. Read them alone. Fix them until they're a poem.

Daily for two weeks and the voice stops being borrowed.
